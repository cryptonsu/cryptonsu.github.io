Keep Both Sides of a Cross-Network Crypto Transfer

A cross-network transfer creates evidence on more than one chain; keep the source and destination records, bridge status, and the details that connect them.

By Crypto Readout EditorialOct 5, 20263 min read#e7b0bb

A cross-network transfer creates a record on the sending network and, if completed, a separate record on the receiving network. To explain what happened, keep both records and the details that connect them: the networks, assets, amounts, wallet addresses, and transfer status. A single transaction hash usually describes one transaction, not the whole journey.

How does a cross-network transfer work?

A cross-network transfer passes through several steps. First, a wallet submits a transaction on the source network. The transaction may lock or burn an asset, or send it to a contract that handles the transfer. A bridge or other transfer system then relays information or value. Finally, a transaction on the destination network releases, mints, or otherwise delivers an asset to a receiving address.

Each step can leave a different record. The source transaction hash identifies the transaction submitted on the first network. The destination transaction hash identifies a later transaction on the second. A bridge may also show a message ID, transfer ID, or status linking the two. These identifiers are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Think of the two transaction hashes as receipts from opposite ends of a trip. Each proves an event on its own network; the bridge record helps show how the events belong together. For a walkthrough of a specific example, see this fuller account of Polygon Bridge transfers in both directions. The same record-keeping principle applies across transfer systems, even when their steps and labels differ.

Which records should I keep?

Keep enough information to identify the transfer, trace its progress, and reconcile what left with what arrived. Save the original details when you start the transfer, then add the destination evidence when it appears. A useful record includes:

  • The date and time, source and destination network names, and the sending and receiving wallet addresses.
  • The asset and amount sent, plus the asset and amount received. Record the token as well as its displayed name or symbol, since names alone may not uniquely identify it.
  • The source and destination transaction hashes, and any bridge message ID or transfer ID shown by the interface.
  • The transfer status and any fee shown, including whether the destination transaction completed or remains pending.

Keep transaction details in their original form where possible. A copied hash or link to a network explorer can help you retrieve the underlying record later. A screenshot can preserve what the bridge interface showed at the time, but it should support the identifiers rather than replace them. Interfaces can change, and a displayed status may not capture every later update.

How can I tell whether the transfer is complete?

Check for evidence on the destination network before treating the transfer as received. A submitted source transaction shows that an action was requested or recorded there; by itself, it does not show that the destination transaction succeeded. Open the destination transaction in an explorer for that network and compare its recipient, asset, and amount with the details you saved.

If the bridge still marks the transfer as pending, keep that status with the source record and check for a destination transaction or updated bridge message later. Some systems require additional steps, and status labels vary. Do not assume that a source hash alone proves delivery, or that a matching amount proves the two records are linked.

For routine tracking, the clearest record is one entry that holds both transaction hashes alongside the shared transfer ID, if available. This makes it easier to explain what left, what arrived, and how the two network records relate.
