Why bridges deduct gas on the destination chain

Destination-chain gas deductions cover the cost of completing a transfer after it reaches another network, and may reduce the quoted token amount or appear separately.

By Crypto Readout EditorialOct 16, 2026 · 22:45 UTC4 min read#45f887

A destination-chain gas deduction pays for the transaction that completes a cross-chain transfer on the receiving network. The source transaction starts the process and costs gas there; a relayer, executor or other delivery mechanism later submits a transaction on the destination chain. The bridge estimates that second cost and accounts for it in the quote, often by reducing the tokens delivered.

The moving parts are the source chain, where the transfer begins; the destination chain, where the recipient gets the asset; and the relayer, which may advance funds or submit the destination transaction. Validators on each chain process transactions and receive that chain’s gas fees. For a separate example of how QuickSwap handles treasury transfers, see QuickSwap’s treasury transfer mechanics.

How is destination-chain gas deducted?

The bridge or route provider first estimates the destination transaction’s gas use and the price of gas on that network. It then converts that cost into the fee currency used by the route. If the user sends a token such as USDC, the quote can subtract an estimated fee from the amount delivered, even though gas on the destination chain is paid in its native token.

That conversion is an accounting step. The recipient may receive fewer USDC, while the relayer uses its own native tokens to pay the destination network. The relayer is later compensated through the transfer’s fee or settlement process. The deduction therefore does not necessarily mean that native gas coins were taken from the recipient’s wallet.

A quote can show the costs in different places. Some providers fold the relayer’s destination gas cost into a relay fee or the quoted output. Others display a separate native fee that the user pays on the source chain in addition to the token amount. Those are different payment paths for covering a destination transaction, so the fee label alone does not tell you what the recipient will receive.

What changes the deduction?

The estimate depends on the destination chain’s gas price and the work required to complete the transfer. A simple token delivery may need less execution than a transfer that also triggers a swap or contract call. Route design matters too: a relayer may fill the destination side directly, while another bridge may use a message and a separate executor.

The quote is an estimate made before the destination transaction is submitted. Network conditions can change between the quote and execution. Providers may include a margin, refresh the quote, or handle a shortfall under their own rules. There is no single destination-gas formula that applies to every bridge.

When comparing routes, check these parts of the quote:

  • Expected output: the amount the recipient should get after fees.
  • Fee currency: whether costs are deducted from the transferred token or added as a native-token payment.
  • Destination execution: whether the route only delivers the asset or also runs a swap or other call.
  • Quote timing: whether the provider requires a fresh quote before signing.

Does the recipient need native gas?

It depends on what happens after delivery. If a relayer submits the destination transaction and the transfer ends with tokens in the recipient’s wallet, the recipient generally does not need native gas just to receive them. To move or spend those tokens afterward, the recipient will need the destination chain’s native gas token.

If the transfer triggers a follow-up swap or contract call, the route must account for the gas needed to execute that action too. A failed follow-up call can leave the bridged asset delivered while the extra action remains incomplete; the recovery path depends on the bridge and application.

The useful comparison is the recipient’s final usable balance, not just the headline fee. Read the output amount, identify any separate native payment, and confirm whether the quote includes the action you expect on the destination chain. A gas deduction is the cost of completing that step, expressed through the route’s chosen fee mechanism.
