How Cross-Chain Minimum Output Guarantees Work

A minimum output sets the least amount a destination-chain swap may deliver; if the route cannot meet it before expiry, the transaction should fail instead.

By Crypto Readout EditorialOct 16, 2026 · 01:33 UTC4 min read#c89985

A cross-chain minimum output guarantee sets the least amount of tokens a transaction must deliver on its destination chain for the transaction to count as successful. The user or routing service sets that threshold before execution. If the route cannot meet it within the transaction’s deadline, the swap should revert or stop rather than complete at a worse rate.

How does a cross-chain minimum output work?

The process starts with a source-chain transaction. It authorizes a contract to take or lock the input tokens and includes instructions for the destination chain: which token to deliver, where to send it, and the minimum acceptable amount. That amount is usually denominated in the destination token, not in dollars.

A bridge or cross-chain messaging system then carries the instruction across. Depending on the route, a bridge may lock tokens on one chain and release corresponding tokens on another, or a liquidity provider may pay the recipient on the destination chain and settle the balance separately. A relayer, solver or destination-chain contract may handle the next step: swapping the received or released tokens into the requested asset.

At execution, the destination swap compares its actual output with the minimum. If the output meets or exceeds the threshold, the contract can send the tokens to the recipient. If it falls short, the swap should fail under the route’s rules. For bridge-specific checks that this paragraph only touches, see the manta bridge guide.

The threshold therefore protects one part of the route: the amount delivered by the swap. It does not by itself guarantee that every cross-chain step will finish, that a bridge is secure, or that the delivered token will keep a particular market value.

How should you choose the minimum output?

Choose a minimum that reflects the least destination-token amount you are willing to accept after the route’s expected fees and price movement. A quote gives an estimated output; the minimum sets a boundary below which execution should not proceed. The gap between them is the slippage allowance.

A tighter threshold leaves less room for the market price to move while the transaction waits for confirmation and destination execution. That can protect the quoted rate, but it also makes failure more likely. A looser threshold gives the route more room to complete, but permits a lower output. The practical choice depends on the asset, trade size, pool liquidity and how long the route may take.

Before signing, check these parts of the quote:

  • Input and output tokens: Confirm the token and chain on each side. A token’s name or ticker alone may not identify the asset contract.
  • Minimum amount: Read the destination-token quantity, not just the estimated dollar value or exchange rate.
  • Fees: Check which bridge, relayer and swap costs are already reflected in the quoted output and threshold.
  • Deadline and failure path: See how long the instruction remains valid and what happens to funds if the destination swap cannot execute.

What does the guarantee cover, and what can still go wrong?

The guarantee is only as strong as the contracts and route that enforce it. A minimum output can prevent a supported swap from delivering less than the stated token amount, but it cannot guarantee that the bridge or messaging system will remain available, that the destination transaction will be included promptly, or that the token itself will retain its price.

Failure handling also varies. Some routes can return or make source-side funds reclaimable after a failed destination action; others may require a separate recovery step or wait. Read the route’s stated process before approving a transfer, especially when the source transaction is irreversible on its chain.

Think of the minimum as a limit written into the delivery instruction: it defines the worst output the route is allowed to accept, much like a limit on a trade. The useful takeaway is simple: compare the quoted output with the minimum, understand the fees and failure path, and set the threshold according to the amount you are prepared to receive.
