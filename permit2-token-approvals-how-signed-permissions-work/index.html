Permit2 Token Approvals: How Signed Permissions Work

How Permit2 works for token approvals: approve Permit2 once, then use signed permissions to set spending limits, expiries and one-time transfers.

By Crypto Readout EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 07:32 UTC4 min read#6d3076

Permit2 separates a token’s on-chain approval from each app’s spending permission: you approve the token to Permit2, then sign an EIP-712 message that lets a specific spender use a defined amount under stated conditions. That means an app can request permission without asking you to submit a new ERC-20 approval transaction each time.

What happens when you approve with Permit2?

First, your wallet sends a regular ERC-20 approve transaction that gives Permit2 permission to spend the token. This approval is usually made once per token per chain. Permit2 is deployed by Uniswap Labs at 0x000000000022D473030F116dDEE9F6B43aC78BA3 on many EVM chains; confirm the address and network before approving.

Next, you sign an EIP-712 message. The app presents the permission, and Permit2 checks the signature and its fields when the app submits it. Depending on the Permit2 mode, the contract stores a limited allowance or authorizes a single transfer. The spender can then pull tokens within those limits.

With AllowanceTransfer, a signed permit creates an allowance inside Permit2 for a spender. A PermitSingle covers one token; a PermitBatch can cover several. The permission includes the spender, token amount and expiration, along with signature timing and replay-protection details. The app can use the allowance until it runs out or expires.

SignatureTransfer is for a one-time transfer. The signed permission sets the token and permitted amount, and includes a nonce and deadline. Permit2 checks that the signature is valid, the deadline has not passed and the nonce has not been used. The transfer then happens; no continuing Permit2 allowance is stored.

For a Jumper bridge route, the app may request a Permit2 signature instead of a second approval transaction, depending on the token and chain. A pending token approval can still hold up the setup, and unsticking a stalled approval explains how to speed up or cancel one. On Jumper bridge, the route’s requested spender and amount still need to match what you intend to authorize.

Read the permission before you sign

Say you want to move a token through a route that needs 100 units. You have already approved Permit2 to spend that token. The route asks for an AllowanceTransfer permit naming its spender, a limit of 100 and an expiration. You sign it; Permit2 records that allowance, and the spender can pull up to 100 units before it expires.

Now suppose the route requests a SignatureTransfer instead. You sign a one-time permission for the permitted amount, with a nonce and deadline. Permit2 checks those values and the signature when the transfer is submitted. Once used, the nonce cannot authorize the same transfer again.

A common mistake is to expect a Permit2 signature to be a harmless login or preview. In reality, signing a PermitSingle or PermitBatch can grant spending power without an on-chain transaction or gas charge for the signature itself. The spender, amount and expiry are real permissions; read them before signing.

Permit2 has two revocation layers. To remove one spender’s stored AllowanceTransfer permission, use Permit2’s approve-to-zero or lockdown option for that token and spender. To cut off every spender that relies on Permit2 for a token, revoke the token’s ERC-20 approval to Permit2 in the token contract or wallet. The first action changes one Permit2 allowance; the second removes Permit2’s underlying access altogether.

Related

How does Permit2 differ from EIP-2612 permit?

EIP-2612 lets a token accept a signed permit to set its ERC-20 allowance, if that token implements the standard. Permit2 is a separate contract: the token first needs an ERC-20 approval to Permit2, and then Permit2 can manage app-specific permissions through AllowanceTransfer or authorize one-time transfers through SignatureTransfer.

Why does Permit2 still ask for one approval first?

Permit2 needs the token’s on-chain allowance before it can move that token. The initial approval gives Permit2 that access; later signatures tell Permit2 which spender may use it, for how much and for how long. Revoke the spender’s Permit2 allowance to limit one app, or revoke the token approval to Permit2 to shut off all such access.
