Why Cross-Chain Transfers Take Time

A cross-chain transfer has separate source-chain, bridge and destination-chain steps; each adds time, so users can locate a delay before retrying or sending again.

By Crypto Readout EditorialOct 15, 2026 · 18:57 UTC4 min read#891f3a

A cross-chain transfer takes as long as its slowest required step: the source chain must confirm the transfer, the bridge must process it, and the destination chain must record the result. Those steps happen on two blockchains and often involve a service or contract between them. A wallet may show “pending” during any one of them, so the label alone does not identify the delay.

What happens during a cross-chain transfer?

First, the user selects a source network, a destination network, an asset and an amount. The wallet prepares a transaction on the source chain, usually sending tokens to a bridge contract or to a bridge service that coordinates the transfer. The source chain’s validators or miners then include that transaction in a block.

Next, the bridge waits for enough source-chain confirmation to treat the deposit as valid. The bridge may use its own validators, a relayer, or a contract that verifies information about the source chain. Once the bridge accepts the deposit, it triggers the destination step. For a simple lock-and-mint bridge, tokens are held on the source chain and a corresponding token is created on the destination chain. Other bridges use liquidity already available on the destination chain, then settle between chains separately.

These are different designs, and their timing differs too. A transfer through Acrossbridge, for example, involves a bridge mechanism between networks; see how Acrossbridge moves crypto between chains for that specific explanation. The general point is that a wallet’s send transaction is only the first part of a cross-chain transfer.

Why do some transfers take longer than others?

Each stage has its own wait. The source chain may take time to include the transaction, especially when the network is busy or the fee is too low for prompt inclusion. After inclusion, the bridge may wait for additional confirmations. More confirmations can reduce the chance that a source-chain block is reversed, but waiting for them adds time.

The bridge’s own processing also matters. A relayer has to observe the source event and submit or authorize the destination action. A bridge that waits for a set of validators to attest to a deposit adds that coordination step. Then the destination chain must include the release, mint or swap transaction in a block. Congestion or a low transaction fee can slow that final step as well.

Liquidity-based bridges have another moving part: available funds on the destination chain. If the bridge cannot immediately provide the requested asset there, it may need to wait for liquidity or use a different route. A displayed estimate is therefore a guide, not a promise. It depends on network conditions and the bridge’s current process.

Think of the transfer like a parcel moving through three depots. The sender’s depot records the parcel, a carrier verifies and routes it, and the destination depot records delivery. A delay at one depot does not mean the parcel has vanished; it means the next stage cannot finish yet.

How can you tell where a transfer is delayed?

Check the transaction status on both networks, using the transaction hash or the bridge’s transfer page. The useful distinction is whether the source transaction is confirmed, whether the bridge has processed that deposit, and whether the destination transaction has completed. A wallet may keep showing a generic pending state even after the source transaction is final.

  • If the source transaction is not included, check its status and fee on the source network.
  • If it is confirmed but the bridge has not processed it, check the bridge’s transfer status and stated confirmation requirement.
  • If the bridge reports completion but the destination wallet is unchanged, look for the destination transaction and confirm the wallet is set to the right network.
  • If the destination transaction failed, check the bridge’s instructions before attempting another transfer.

Use the exact network and token shown by the bridge when starting a transfer. Similar token names can exist on different chains, and a token created by a bridge may not be interchangeable with the original asset everywhere. Keep the transaction hashes and avoid starting a second transfer just because the first still says pending; first identify which stage has finished and which has not.

For most first-time users, the clearest way to judge timing is to follow the three records: source transaction, bridge processing and destination transaction. The bridge cannot complete its part before the source deposit is confirmed, and the destination wallet cannot show the received asset until the destination chain records the result.
