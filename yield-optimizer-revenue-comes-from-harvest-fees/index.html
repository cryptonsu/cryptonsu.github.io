Yield Optimizer Revenue Comes From Harvest Fees

How do yield optimizers make money? They mainly take a performance fee from harvested rewards, then split it among the protocol, contributors and token stakers.

By Crypto Readout EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 04:10 UTC3 min read#5d6bf4

Yield optimizers make money mainly by taking a performance fee from rewards they harvest and compound. The fee is cut from yield earned, so it comes out of the reward flow rather than the depositor’s original principal.

A vault earns rewards from a farm or other DeFi strategy. At harvest, it claims those rewards, takes the fee share, and reinvests the remainder. The Beefy app’s vaults show this process clearly: Beefy app automates the harvest and compounding across supported strategies.

Harvest fees pay the people and protocol behind a vault

On a Beefy app harvest, the strategy claims reward tokens, cuts the performance fee, and divides that fee among the treasury, strategist, harvest caller and BIFI stakers. The rest of the rewards is converted as needed and added back to the vault, increasing the assets working for depositors. Beefy’s [fee documentation](https://docs.beefy.finance/ecosystem/beefy-bulletins/beefy-finance-fees-breakdown) describes those recipients.

Each recipient has a role in keeping the vault running. The treasury receives protocol revenue. The strategist who developed the vault receives a share for maintaining or contributing the strategy. BIFI stakers receive a share through the BIFI Pool and related incentive programs. The harvest caller gets a fee for triggering the transaction.

That caller fee helps keep vaults compounding without requiring the protocol to manually trigger every harvest. Harvesting costs gas, so an outside bot can compare the caller reward with the transaction cost and call when the reward makes the expense worthwhile. The fee pays for the work and the gas; it does not guarantee that every harvest is profitable for every caller.

Imagine a vault has accumulated ten reward tokens since its last harvest. The harvest transaction claims them, sets aside the performance fee and routes that cut to its recipients. The remaining tokens are swapped or paired as the strategy requires, then deposited back into the vault. The depositor’s balance grows through compounding, while the fee recipients are paid from the harvested rewards.

Fee type and reward volume determine revenue

A performance fee applies to yield earned; a management fee charges against assets held. The distinction matters because a vault can hold a large deposit while producing little new yield, or generate substantial rewards from a smaller pool. Yearn Finance has charged both management and performance fees on vaults, with the exact structure depending on the vault; its [fee documentation](https://docs.yearn.fi/developers/v2/vault-factory/) explains that model.

Some optimizers may also charge deposit or withdrawal fees. Those apply when a user enters or exits a vault, while a management fee accrues against assets over time. A performance fee is tied to harvest profits. Each fee type creates a different charge point, so users should check the fee details for the specific vault they plan to use.

For an optimizer funded mainly by performance fees, revenue scales with the total rewards harvested. That depends on the vault’s TVL and the farm’s reward rate: more assets can earn more rewards at a given rate, while a higher reward rate can increase yield on the same assets. If rewards fall or fewer assets are deposited, the fee base can shrink too.

The business model follows the harvest cycle: farm rewards are claimed, a fee share is split among the protocol and its contributors, and the balance is compounded for depositors. Since the fee is tied to realized rewards, the volume of rewards harvested—not simply the amount deposited—drives performance-fee revenue.
