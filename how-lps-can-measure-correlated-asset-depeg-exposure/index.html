How LPs Can Measure Correlated-Asset Depeg Exposure

A depeg can turn a market-neutral pool into a one-sided position; LPs can assess exposure by tracing asset links, pool mechanics and exit liquidity.

By Crypto Readout EditorialSep 29, 20263 min read#8f7abd

Correlated-asset depeg exposure is the loss a liquidity provider can face when two assets expected to track each other stop trading at the same value. In an automated market maker, trades change the pool’s reserves and price; as traders buy the stronger asset and sell the weaker one, the pool holds more of the asset losing value. Fees may offset part of that loss, but they do not restore the peg. The risk begins with the assets’ dependencies, then passes through the pool’s pricing curve and the LP’s ability to exit. Why can correlated assets depeg?

Two tokens can move together in ordinary markets while relying on different systems to hold their prices together. A wrapped token depends on custody or a bridge to represent another asset. A liquid staking token depends on the underlying staking position, redemption process and market demand. A stablecoin may depend on reserves, an issuer and access to redemption. Each mechanism can fail or become difficult to use on its own.

Correlation describes how prices have moved; it does not guarantee that holders can exchange one token for the other at a fixed rate. Shared exposure can also make a break more severe. If several tokens depend on the same collateral, custodian, chain or market maker, stress in that dependency can affect them at once. A pair that looks diversified by token name may still concentrate risk in one redemption route.

What happens to an LP position during a depeg?

A depeg changes the pool’s reserve mix as traders act on the price difference. In a constant-product pool, the pricing rule keeps the product of the two reserve quantities roughly constant, so buying one token removes it from the pool while selling the other adds to its reserve. An LP who withdraws receives a share of those reserves, not the original deposit mix.

Some pools use a stable-swap curve designed to reduce trading slippage when assets remain close in value. That design can make swaps efficient near the target price, but it cannot guarantee that the assets will recover or that outside markets will support the peg. The pool’s curve, fee rate and liquidity depth shape how quickly its reserves shift. A narrow concentrated-liquidity position can also stop earning fees once the market price moves outside its chosen range.

The key distinction is between a brief price gap and a lasting impairment. A temporary gap may close before an LP exits. A lasting depeg can leave the pool increasingly weighted toward the weaker token. Fees compensate for providing liquidity; they are not insurance against that outcome.

How should LPs assess depeg exposure?

Assess the path from a token’s backing to an LP’s withdrawal. Check the pool’s rules and the assets’ dependencies before estimating returns. A practical review asks:

  • What keeps each price near its target? Identify collateral, redemption rights, bridge or custodian, and any market-making support.
  • Where do the dependencies overlap? Shared collateral, issuers, chains or redemption routes can expose both sides to the same failure.
  • How would the pool rebalance? Review its curve, fee, liquidity depth and, for concentrated liquidity, the price range.
  • Could you exit under stress? Consider outside-market depth, withdrawal limits and whether redemption still works when demand surges.

Compare the pool’s fee income with a scenario in which one asset trades below its expected value and stays there. Include the value of the resulting reserve mix, not just the quoted yield. For most LPs, the better choice is a pool whose asset dependencies and exit route they can explain clearly. Similar tickers or recent price correlation are not enough.
